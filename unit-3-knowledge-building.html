The standards in this unit come from the California Career Technical Education Model Curriculum Standards: Manufacturing and Product Development, Pathway C, Welding and Materials Joining (California Department of Education, 2017). Open the standards (PDF, CDE).

Reading a standard

The standards in the Welding and Materials Joining pathway are about applying equipment and skills, and about carrying those skills into the workforce. Before you can judge whether a simulator supports a standard, read the standard closely: what it asks a student to do, and what it asks them to produce.

How the standards are organized

The California standards have three layers:

  • Anchor standardsGlossary: Anchor standard. Eleven standards shared by all fifteen industry sectors. Anchor Standard 6.0, Health and Safety, is one of them. You will use it in the safety topic.
  • Pathway standards. Nine for welding, C1.0 to C9.0. Each names a broad area, such as C2.0, processing materials with welding tools and equipment.
  • Performance indicatorsGlossary: Performance indicator. The numbered items under each pathway standard, such as C2.3. These say what a student should be able to do.

This unit calls the indicators “standards,” because that is how most instructors talk about them. Each row of your Alignment Table is one indicator.

Read the verb, then the product

Take C2.3. The verb is use: welding tools and equipment, across several processes, to join parts. The product is “a finished product that meets the standards of the AWS or a similar industry standard.” The verb tells you the skill. The product tells you what counts as proof the skill is there.

What “meets the standards” asks for

Meeting a standard means the student shows proficiency. Depending on the standard, that can be proficiency with tools and equipment, knowledge and vocabulary, welding technique, or print reading.

An industry code makes that proficiency measurable. AWS D1.1/D1.1M:2025, Structural Welding Code – Steel, sets performance standards that come straight from industry, including the tests a welder takes to qualify. Simulator exercises can be built around those same tests and qualification assessments, so practice on the simulator points at a real target.

From simulator to plate

When a student moves from the simulator to live welding, expect a re-orientation phaseGlossary: Re-orientation phase. Heat, spatter, and noise are all new, and each can distract a student and lower their performance at first. The goal is for the student to carry the muscle memoryGlossary: Muscle memory and fundamental movements practiced on the simulator to the plate, while they get used to everything else live welding brings.

That is why a standard like C2.3 matters for this unit. The simulator can build the movements. The finished product, to an industry standard, comes from live welding.

What simulator practice exercises

To rate a standard, compare what it asks for with what students actually do on the simulator. A vendor’s feature list is a place to start, not the answer. You read vendor claims closely in Unit 1; the same habit applies here.

What students practice

Simulator practice usually covers:

  • The four foundational processes: GMAW, SMAW, GTAW, and FCAW
  • The four positions: flat, horizontal, vertical, and overhead
  • Two joint types: fillet and groove

In short, the simulator is built for joining: the hand technique of making a weld, across processes, positions, and joints.

It also builds one habit that matters at the live arc: wearing the helmet. Early on, students can find the helmet cumbersome. Wearing it through every simulator exercise makes it part of their practice before they move to live welding, where helmet use is an absolute must.

What students never do on the simulator

A live coupon takes a significant amount of preparation that the simulator skips:

  • Cleaning and shaping the joint
  • Verifying the joint geometry the code specifies
  • Tack welding the specimen for the weld position
  • Grinding, before and after welding, especially on multipass welds

The simulator also has no heat, spatter, noise, or smoke. And it is used for joining, not cutting. Cutting is a foundational shop skill, practiced alongside simulator welding rather than on it.

Use this when you rate

If what a standard asks for depends on something in the second list, simulator practice cannot fully support it. It may still support part of it, such as the hand technique.

Rating support

Every row of the Alignment Table gets one of three ratings. The rating is a decision about your program, and Unit 4 builds your plan from it.

Three ratings

  • Supported. A student can meet the whole standard through simulator practice.
  • Partially supported. Simulator practice builds part of what the standard asks, usually the hand technique. The rest needs live work. Name the part.
  • Not supported. What the standard asks depends on things the simulator does not present: preparation, real metal, cutting, or testing.

Rate what practice exercises, not the score or the brochure

Two shortcuts lead to the wrong rating.

The simulator score. A passing score shows a student made the movements well in that exercise. Most welding standards ask for proficiency shown on real work, such as a finished product that meets AWS D1.1. A good score is a sign the student is ready to move on. It is not proof the standard is met.

The vendor’s list. “Covers SMAW, GMAW, FCAW, and GTAW” tells you which processes a student can practice. It does not tell you which standards that practice meets.

Instead, use the two lists from the last topic. Ask what the standard asks the student to do and produce, then check each part against what students practice on the simulator and what they never do there.

“Partial” means naming the part

“Partially supported” on its own tells Unit 4 nothing. Your plan needs to know which part the simulator handles and which part must be taught live. Write the part in a few words, for example “hand technique in all four positions; not joint preparation or the finished part.”

If every row of a table says “partially supported,” the table has not made any decisions yet. Some standards will be fully supported, some partly, and some not at all.

An example of a “not supported” rating: C4.2, “Apply the processes used for finishing welded materials.” Finishing means grinding and cleaning real steel after welding. The simulator leaves nothing to finish, so practice on it cannot support this standard.

Safety that must be taught live

The simulator has no arc, heat, spatter, or smoke. That makes it a safe place to practice, and it also means simulator practice teaches almost none of the safety habits live welding needs. A student can arrive at the booth confident in their technique and new to the hazards. Every standard you rate that involves live work carries safety requirements, and those are taught at the live arc.

Where the safety requirements come from

The California standards. Pathway C has no safety indicator of its own. Safety sits in Anchor Standard 6.0, Health and Safety, which applies across the whole sector, welding included. Indicator 6.4 names the hazards:

6.4 Set up a work area, or shop, to avoid potential health concerns and safety hazards including but not limited to ergonomics, electrical (shock), wires (tripping), fumes (lung health), noise (hearing loss), fire (burns), and so forth, incorporating ergonomics.

(California Department of Education, 2017, MPD 4)

ANSI Z49.1:2021, Safety in Welding, Cutting, and Allied Processes. The standard’s definition of a welder includes educators and students (clause 2.12). It requires that anyone exposed to welding hazards be trained in how to use protective clothing and equipment, and why (4.6).

What the simulator can start

One habit carries over: wearing the helmet through every exercise, so it is routine before the first live arc. Almost everything else is learned live.

What has to be taught at the live arc

  • Arc radiation. A helmet with a filter lens, plus safety glasses with side shields or goggles underneath (Z49.1, 4.2.1.1).
  • Fumes and gases. Keeping the head out of the fume (5.3). The simulator makes no smoke, so this habit can only start live.
  • Electrical shock. Training to avoid shock (11.4.9).
  • Fire and burns. Clothing chosen so it will not ignite or trap sparks (4.3), and combustibles moved away from the work (6.1).
  • Equipment not in use. Students are often eager to set the electrode holder down the moment they finish a weld. At the live arc, that habit can cause unnecessary arcing or damage to other items, including the electrode holder itself. Z49.1 requires that a holder not in use be placed where it cannot make electrical contact with people or conducting objects (11.4.8).

Before the first live arc

A typical routine before live welding:

  • A PPE check
  • A condition check of the lab equipment and the welding machine
  • An inventory of the tools the job needs, such as pliers, wire brushes, and chipping hammers

Z49.1 also requires that each connection to the machine be checked before welding starts (11.4.2).
